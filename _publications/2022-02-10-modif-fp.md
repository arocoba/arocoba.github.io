Semiconductor random lasers are typically fabricated by introducing disorder directly into the active region, often requiring complex nanofabrication techniques. In this work, we demonstrate a much simpler approach: converting a commercially available Fabry–Pérot laser diode into an electrically driven random laser through controlled femtosecond laser ablation of its output mirror.

The ablation process creates a disordered scattering surface that replaces the conventional optical feedback provided by a smooth mirror. While the back mirror of the laser diode remains unchanged, the modified front facet introduces random phase and amplitude variations, resulting in multimode random lasing emission. The device exhibits a broad Gaussian spectrum with randomly distributed narrow peaks, irregular angular emission patterns, and significantly reduced spatial coherence compared with the original Fabry–Pérot laser.

Experimental measurements reveal the transition from conventional single-mode operation to a random-lasing regime characterized by multiple longitudinal and transverse modes. The observed behaviour is supported by a theoretical model based on disordered cavity feedback, showing how mirror roughness modifies the spectral distribution of the cavity modes.

This work provides a simple and accessible route towards electrically pumped semiconductor random lasers using standard commercial laser diodes and a single post-processing step. The approach opens new possibilities for low-cost random laser sources and for applications requiring low spatial coherence, complex multimode dynamics, and engineered optical disorder.
